We are three AI Product Managers at Northwind People, a fictional tech company in Berlin with about 1,200 employees. We own the AI assistants that HR and employees use. Our onboarding assistant showed a new employee a colleague's salary. In 10 minutes: what happened, how we reasoned about the cause, and what we recommend. Everything in this case is fictional.

Normally the assistant helps new employees in their first weeks. It answers HR questions, such as holidays or salary ranges, and shows each person their own to-do list. It can only read information, never change it. On November 12, a new employee, four days into the job, asked for the salary range of their role. The assistant gave the range, and also a colleague's exact salary and bonus. It even named its source: a document only HR should see. Expected was the public salary range only. Why it matters: private pay data reached the wrong person, it may be a data protection breach, which our data protection officer is checking, and trust in HR and the assistant is at risk. What we did first: we blocked all HR-only documents, kept normal HR answers online, and secured the system logs. The screenshot from the employee is our only hard evidence. The AI model and its instructions were updated one week earlier. That is a lead, not proof. So how could this happen?

We followed three steps. First, facts first: only the screenshot is certain, everything else is an assumption. Second, we followed the path of the question: from the new employee, through the assistant, to the documents, and back as an answer with the leak. Third, at every checkpoint we asked: could the check have failed here? That gives three explanations. A, wrong role: the system thought the new employee was an HR employee, so the access check worked, but with wrong information. B, check skipped: the access check was not used for HR-only documents; the lock was there but not used. C, wrong label: the salary document was marked public, so it was in the wrong drawer. All three are equally uncertain: we have only one example. Our logs record every request. One log entry shows who the system thought the user was, whether the access check ran, and where the document came from, so it tells A, B and C apart. We also search the logs for other affected users. An AI reviewed our thinking and added two ideas: a reused answer from another user, and privacy protected only by instructions to the AI. We kept both as backups. We deliberately did not blame the recent AI update without evidence. So what do we do now?

Our recommendation: keep the assistant running safely, find the real cause in the logs, then make the smallest fix. Now: HR-only documents stay blocked, and the data protection officer reviews the case. Within two days: we analyse the logs, check A, B and C, and search for other leaks. Then: we fix only what the logs confirm: the role, the access check, or the document label. For the future: a leak test before every update, and logs that record every access decision. What we will not do: switch to a smarter AI model. An AI model is not a lock; the system must enforce access rules. How we know it helped, our zero-leak testing: the original question plus 20 test questions about private data must produce zero leaks; new employees still get normal answers, like the public salary range; the logs show no HR-only document in answers to new employees, reviewed weekly for four weeks. If even one leak appears, we block HR data again or pause the assistant. The HR product owner is responsible, together with the data protection officer. Our main lesson: a privacy failure in an AI product is usually a permission or data problem, not an AI problem. Thank you.
